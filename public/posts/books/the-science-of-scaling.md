I've been listening to The Science of Scaling by Dr. Benjamin Hardy and Blake Erickson. The core argument of the book is that companies don't stall because people aren't working hard enough. They stall because leaders are operating from the wrong mental model of growth. According to the authors, a business growing 10-20% a year isn't healthy, it's stagnating. The book lays out a framework that they claim lets companies grow 10-100x within three years.

## My Thoughts

The framework has three parts:

1. **Frame** - set goals so big that your current approach can't possibly get you there.
1. **Floor** - decide what you are no longer willing to accept.
1. **Focus** - simplify the business down to one clear, repeatable path.

### Target Audience

I believe the target audience for this book is successful business owners. The book makes the assumption that a business is already successful. The business already knows how to create its product, how to sell it, and who they're selling to. The business owner must already know how to execute. This focus of this book can then help a business grow. A business that doesn't already know how to execute will not be benefitted by an extreme goal.

### Musings

- Most of this book is focused on anecdotes. Although the title is The "Science" of Scaling, there's not much science in this book. This book relies heavily on anectdotes.
- Likely due to the extensive use of anectdotes, I feel like the core message of this book could be effectively communicated in a two-page essay. The rest of the book is just stories that are used to inspire the reader. I expect this was a conscious decision by the author; the persona of the typical business owner the author is targeting may be more influenced by emotion than I am as an engineer.
- An interesting line from one of the anecdotes: "a CEO must be an elite story-teller". I have observed this to be true as well. A CEO must be able to convince people with money to invest in the company, and that requires being an elite story-teller.


## Change Your Frame

### Impossible Goals

> It is a goal that you do not initially know how to achieve.

An impossible goal is one that you don't know how to reach and that requires capabilities you don't currently have. The authors describe this kind of goal as a "hot knife" that cuts through the noise.

- A linear goal (grow 10% this year) lets you keep all of your current assumptions. You can get there by doing what you're already doing, just a little bit more.
- A 10x goal forces you to question everything. Most of what you're doing today won't get you to 10x, so you're forced to find a different path.
- Try it: take your 10% target and work out what the 1,000% version would look like. Then look at which of your current tasks only make sense for linear growth, and cut them.
- The authors connect this to _prospection_, the brain's ability to imagine the future. The future you're aiming for filters what you pay attention to today. A bigger goal filters more aggressively.

This idea builds on Hardy's earlier book, 10x Is Easier Than 2x.

I think "impossible goal" is worded to be catchy, but the goal obviously can't actually be impossible. In order to achieve a goal, you *must* believe it's possible. Other people might believe it's impossible, but you have to believe there's a way to reach your goal. The type of goal this book is advocating for is one that pushes you to your limit; a goal that mandates total focus and commitment. When this type of goal is set and total committment is given, the results are astounding because most people are unwilling or unable to devote themselves to an extreme goal and do what it takes to realize it.

### Time as a Tool

> Time is not just a clock. It is a tool for making decisions.

Parkinson's Law says that work expands to fill the time available for its completion. The authors flip this around and use aggressive timelines on purpose.

- If you have twelve months to do something, ask what it would look like to do it in one month. A compressed timeline exposes what actually matters and what is just busy work.
- Find "the crux," the core bottleneck. Solving the crux makes other problems easier or makes them disappear entirely. Put the majority of your resources (around 80%) on the crux.
- Skip "Gemini missions." The Apollo program needed the Gemini program to work out the steps before going to the moon. The authors argue that many of the intermediate phases we plan for are unnecessary and can be skipped.
- Use weekly targets instead of quarterly reviews.
- They contrast _linear time_, where the past determines the present, with _holistic time_, where the future you're aiming for shapes the present.

I think that this principle is broadly applicable. Meetings tend to expand to fill the time allotted to them, software timelines tend to expand to fill the time estimated, etc. With these types of practices in mind, I think it's also essential to plan for the future. If the work we do today focuses exclusively on short-term gains, then todays success might undermine tomorrow if we take shortcuts today that will prevent us from innovating tomorrow.

## Raise Your Floor

> The floor is your baseline performance. It is what you are no longer willing to accept.

Your floor is the set of things you will no longer tolerate: bad projects, bad clients, and team members who aren't performing. The authors say your floor should sit above most people's ceiling.

### Fast Folding

The idea of "fast folding" comes from professional poker. Good poker players fold bad hands quickly instead of hoping that the next card will save them. The same applies to projects, clients, and hires.

- Sunk cost bias is what keeps us playing bad hands. The time and money already spent are gone regardless of what you decide next.
- Write down your non-negotiables so that you can recognize a bad hand quickly.
- Screen new leads fast. The book suggests a quick 30-second screen.
- Audit your current projects for anything you're only continuing because of what you've already put into it.
- Make performance visible to everyone.

## Accelerate Your Focus

### Simplicity

> You cannot scale a complex system.

As companies grow, they pile up what the authors call "the elephant": tangled software, too many product lines, and convoluted processes. They contrast the Latin roots of the words: _complexus_ means braided or woven together, and _simplex_ means a single fold.

- Do an 80/20 cut on your products. Keep only what is "best in class." Steve Jobs did this when he returned to Apple and cut the product line down to just a few products.
- Optimize for a single North Star metric. The example they give is podcast downloads.
- Remove layers of management.

### The Protocol

> Take many complex offerings and turn them into a single, simple concept.

The protocol is a single, simple offer that solves a major problem for the customer. It should be a "no-brainer" that a customer can buy without confusion, and simple enough that it can be delivered by others and eventually automated.

To get there, the authors apply Elon Musk's five-step algorithm:

1. Question every requirement.
1. Delete any part or process you can.
1. Simplify and optimize.
1. Accelerate cycle time.
1. Automate.

The order matters. It's a waste to automate a process that shouldn't exist in the first place.

### Super Whos

> Rock stars will be inspired; average employees will want the status quo.

You can't scale if every decision has to go through you. The authors call this the "1,000 helpers trap": a leader surrounded by people who can only help the leader execute, rather than people who own outcomes themselves.

Instead, hire "Super Whos," high performers who are motivated by impossible goals. The name is a nod to Hardy's earlier book, Who Not How.

- Lead, don't manage.
- Use the impossible goal as a filter when hiring. Super Whos will be excited by it. Average performers will be intimidated by it.
- Measure results, not hours.
- The authors claim that a top performer can produce 100x to 10,000x the results of an average employee.

## Some Skepticism

- Many of the claims, like 10-100x growth in three years, come from the authors' own clients. Survivorship bias is a real concern here. We hear about the companies that grew 50x, not the ones that set a 50x goal and went under. It is a very real risk that focusing on the wrong thing and aggressively pursuing the wrong result would lead to failure. It's also a real risk that even when focused on the "right" thing, that external factors such as the market changing out from underneath you can inhibit reaching your goal. I suppose in any scenario you could change your goal, but it's challenging to have the required focus, determination, and overcome-every-obstacle mentality to pursue an impossible goal while also being open to changing your goal under circumstances where either the goal is misguided or the goal is no longer possible. An honest, grounded, periodic check-in would probably be the most useful way to steer the ship while navigating a company to determine if you're going in the right direction and/or if there are any unforseen obstacles that require a change in course, whether a detour or in extreme cases a new destination.
- There aren't many hard numbers to back up the bigger claims.
- Some ideas, like "a business is either scaling aggressively or dying slowly," feel more like motivational framing than science, despite the title. This seems most likely to be true for technology companies, and less likely to be true for a manual labor company, such as a plumbing company.
